02

Eight decisions already made

None of these were designed up front. Each one is in the schema because a specific state broke the version before it. That history is worth knowing before anyone proposes undoing one.

Every figure carries a page citation, and the citation is checked quote, page_start, citation_verified

Nothing is loaded without a verbatim quote and the page it sits on, and the loader searches that page before writing. A criterion whose quote cannot be found is worse than a missing one. All 1,935 currently verify.

This is the whole promise. Everything else is bookkeeping around it.

Scoring universes that cannot be pooled are tracks track, track_totals

Twenty states run more than one. Michigan scores urban and rural applications on separate 44-point scales and a project is in exactly one; its supportive-housing criteria apply only to supportive-housing projects. One number could not describe that state honestly.

Michigan has four tracks. Collapsing them would have invented a 175-point scale no applicant faces.

Criteria that cannot both be earned are grouped exclusivity_groups

Two rules: max_one, where a menu contributes only its largest option, and capped_sum, where a family of items is capped below their sum.

Maryland lists three Community Context options at 16 points each. Summed naively that state reads 253 against its stated 221.

Tiers say whether they are alternatives or components tier_mode

260 criteria are alternative (only the best band applies), 89 are additive (the bands sum to the maximum), 58 are mixed. The two look identical in the data and the arithmetic depends entirely on which it is.

Inferred by arithmetic for the states coded before the field existed. Those inferences still want confirming.

Not every state scores in points scoring_unit

1,322 criteria are in points. Utah's 16 are weighted — a raw score times a category weight, so its largest criterion is worth 5,000. Vermont ranks in checkmarks. Totals must never mix units.

Storing Utah's 5,000 in the same column as Ohio's 100 without this would make them look comparable.

Deductions are criteria, and sit outside stated totals is_negative

94 rows are penalties. States exclude them from their own stated maxima, and so do we. Wyoming's deductions reach −1,510 against a 495-point scale.

Michigan's section D totals 11, which is its two positive criteria and none of its four deductions. Independent confirmation of the rule.

Points decided elsewhere say so detail_external

64 criteria set a weight but not a test — an opportunity index, a certification, a separate exhibit. The QAP says how much it is worth and something else decides who earns it.

Ohio's whole 100-point system is three index-driven criteria, two of which point at an Urban Institute index published separately.

Contradictions are recorded, not resolved note, track_totals.note

Where a document disagrees with itself, both readings go in the note with the page to check. Five states currently compute a total different from the one they state — Indiana, Nebraska, Nevada, Tennessee and West Virginia — and each has a documented reason.

Delaware states 234 on p.34 and 231 on p.52. Tennessee's QAP says outright that its criteria "are not intended to allow an Applicant to claim the maximum 100 points".

03

What it already shows

Three things visible in the data now, before any review.

Scales are not comparable, and the spread is larger than people expect

West Virginia's scale is 993 points. Michigan's base track is 78. Any claim of the form “state X weights Y heavily” has to be normalised first, or it is measuring scale, not priority.

Sixteen of 47 states score all eight categories

The rest leave at least one unscored, which is a policy choice rather than an oversight. Ohio scores in only two: its entire system is a neighbourhood opportunity index, a housing needs index, and credit per unit.

Some scoring cannot be audited from the plan at all

64 criteria hand the actual test to a document the plan only names. If you want to know why a project scored what it did, the QAP is not sufficient for those points.

04

Six things to decide

This is the agenda. Each has a concrete proposal attached, and for three of them there is a working local database you can open rather than imagine.

1. Review has a table for decisions and nowhere to record what is waiting

The reviews table allows accepted, edited, rejected, skipped and adjudicated — all outcomes. There is no “pending”, because a row only exists once someone has reviewed. So the queue is currently implicit: everything not yet in reviews. That is workable at 33 items and not at 1,935.

33 criteria have extraction notes that explicitly ask for a second reader. They are the obvious first pass.

Decide: does the queue live in the database, or in whatever tracker the team already uses? And what counts as “reviewed” — the citation, the number, or the judgement?

2. The eight categories are too coarse for the questions people will ask

They were built so totals add up, and they do that well. But “which states score broadband?” cannot be answered from them, because broadband sits inside Design, accessibility & sustainability with forty other things. Today that question is answered by text search, which misses any state that says “fixed route stop” and never “transit”.

Decide: do we add a second, many-to-many concept vocabulary? If so, who owns the word list — and is it fixed up front or grown as states are read?

3. Cross-state comparison needs one agreed denominator

Share of the state's own track total is the obvious normaliser, and it is computable now for 1,094 of 1,156 scored criteria. The other 62 have no denominator because their state states no total at all — five coded states publish no scale.

Decide: for a state that states no total, do we normalise against our computed total, or leave those criteria out of every comparison?

4. The corpus is one cycle deep

The schema already supports several cycles per state — cycle_start, governs_round, is_most_recent — but only four states currently hold more than one cycle's documents. Change over time is the question most people will ask of this data, and we cannot answer it yet.

Decide: is historical depth in scope? If so, how many cycles back, and is it worth more than reviewing what we have?

5. There is no independent check on the coding

prior_codings and prior_matches exist to hold a second, independent reading and record where it agrees. Both are empty. Every figure here was produced by one pass, verified mechanically against its page but not read twice.

Decide: a blind second coding of a sample, or a full human review of everything? The first gives a defensible error rate; the second gives confidence but no measurement.

6. Sources are drifting away from PDFs

Kentucky publishes its scoring only as a spreadsheet, and it is now read as a document whose pages are sheets, with citations naming an exact cell. Maryland's lives in a Program Guide, Michigan's and Minnesota's in separate workbooks, Washington's in a policies document.

Decide: do we keep absorbing formats, or draw a line and record some states as out of scope for the pipeline?

05

There is a working proposal you can open

Questions 1, 2 and 3 are implemented against the real 47 states in a local database, so the meeting can look at them rather than discuss them in the abstract.

python -m qapdb.propose        # writes data/qap_proposed.db
sqlite3 data/qap_proposed.db

It copies the live database and adds three things, touching nothing in the original:

AdditionWhat it doesSize
concepts, criterion_concepts A many-to-many concept vocabulary, seeded with 12 worked examples. Every link is marked suggested, never confirmed — the patterns that proposed them over- and under-match on purpose, so the shape is arguable. 561 links
v_criterion_share Each criterion as a percentage of its own track total, using the stated total where one exists. Makes “who weights transit most” a query rather than an argument. 1,094 rows
review_queue What is waiting and why, which reviews has no place for. Seeded with the 33 criteria whose own notes ask for a second reader. 33 rows
A sample of what it answers. Ranking transit criteria by share of their own scale puts New Jersey first at 22.7% of a 22-point track, then California at 13.8% and Kentucky at 13.3%. On raw points California's 15 would have led and New Jersey's 5 would have looked trivial.

The file is gitignored. It is a proposal, not a migration, and nothing downstream reads it.

Status. 47 of 50 states are scored. Oregon, Montana and Florida run no point system, so the corpus is complete rather than short. Nothing has been through second-reader review.
